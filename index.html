What I doI lead design teams inside enterprise product organisations. My work sits where product strategy, research and craft meet — strategy a VP will sign off on, research rigorous enough to overturn a roadmap, craft that survives contact with engineering.

What I actually get hired forMaking design worth arguing with — and giving a team the evidence to win the argument.

I came into this through building things and never left that part behind. Fifteen years in, I still open the file. What changed is scope: the interesting problem stopped being the screen and became the conditions that produce good screens.

As a lead I'm specific rather than gentle. Designers get autonomy over their problems and a very clear standard for the output — and I name the cost of a decision before we pay it, not in the retro.

Coaching keeps me honest. Most design problems are really comprehension problems: someone senior doesn't yet see why this matters, and no amount of craft fixes that.

The problemExecutives spent 3+ hours a week compiling compliance data by hand. Over 60% of the metrics they received were irrelevant to their decisions, and there was no drill-down by region, so nobody could find a root cause.

The decision I'd defend in any interviewResearch showed three business segments needed genuinely different things, and a single universal dashboard would have failed at least one of them. I chose three segment-specific views. It cost two extra weeks of design time; it removed post-launch adoption friction — and rework after launch is never two weeks. I priced that trade-off before making it.

What I got wrongI used a scatter plot for agent risk and participants couldn't read it. I replaced it with a ranked table with score badges: all eight then identified high-risk agents in under ten seconds. The scatter plot was the more interesting chart. The table was the correct one.

The problemCompliance officers spent 2 hours 45 minutes per assessment across four disconnected systems. That's a capacity problem and a regulatory exposure at once — a longer queue in AML is not an inconvenience.

The insight came from watching, not askingInterviews said the system was fine. Observation said otherwise: I watched an experienced officer spend 18 minutes switching systems to validate three transactions. Her verdict: "it's fine, I'm used to it." These users hadn't normalised a bad tool — they'd lost the ability to imagine a better one. You cannot gather requirements from people in that state; you have to show them something.

A trade-off I lost, and why that was correctI planned real-time push notifications; legal flagged alert fatigue. We settled on an in-app notification centre plus an email digest. The weaker interaction, the right decision — in this domain, a missed alert is not a UX problem.

What outlasted the projectThree departments adopted our design system, two teams adopted the feedback ritual I introduced, and the Product Owner later told me the workshops changed how he writes requirements. That last one isn't a metric — it's the reason I want to run a design function rather than a project.

The diagnosisTwo layers, because the two failures are different failures. At product level: you log in and leave knowing exactly what you knew before — nothing ranked, nothing escalated, a bill due tomorrow looks identical to a coffee from last week. At interface level: no hierarchy, no typographic scale, no grid, not a single chart.

Two ways to run it, priced honestlyScenario A — classic discovery, engineers involved from day one, 11 weeks to MVP, low risk. Scenario B — AI-compressed discovery, 4 weeks to MVP, medium risk. Naming the risk level is the point. A leadership team can choose between these two. It cannot choose between one option and a preference.

Where the business case livesThe AI assistant isn't the feature; it's the instrument. Every question a user asks is a signal about intent and unmet need, fed back into product decisions. That loop compounds — and a competitor can't copy it by copying the interface.

The brief that would have failedStakeholders asked for two things at once: keep every field from the legacy interface, and add "a better look and feel." Keeping all the data would have reproduced the exact usability problem we were hired to remove.

What I did insteadI designed around progressive disclosure, so the interface could stay minimal while leaving room for data to be added later without re-cluttering it. That reframed the argument with stakeholders: not "which fields do we cut", but "which fields does a dispatcher need in the first three seconds".

Research that didn't rely on opinionsOne-to-one sessions and observation, combined with server-log analysis of which interface elements were genuinely used. The logs settled several disagreements that interviews alone would have left open — the only quantitative research in this portfolio.

Leadership

How I run a design team, and the habits that made it work on an award-winning AI platform.

I let people do what they're best atWork assigned against individual strengths, not availability — then I stay out of the solution. Designers own how they solve it; I own whether the bar was met.

I take the leadership conversations so the team doesn't have toI translate between director-level vision and what the team can realistically ship this quarter, and bring decisions back already made rather than passing pressure downwards.

I hold strategy and detail at the same timeI'll challenge a roadmap in the morning and review component spacing in the afternoon. A design leader who can't judge the craft can't defend the team's work when someone senior questions it.

I give feedback early, specific, and out loudDirect feedback raised the consistency of what we shipped more than any process change did. Kind and accurate are the same thing when the timing is right.

Autonomy over the problem, no negotiation on the standard.
